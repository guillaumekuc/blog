Junior tech hiring has frozen. AI-generated code ships in production. Audiences can't distinguish human art from machine output. Frontier model costs fall an order of magnitude per year.

What is happening underneath appears to me to be subtler than a binary "AI takes jobs" vs. "AI is just a tool". Orthogonal ideas about the value of work are colliding and restructuring in real-time: scale, mastery, discovery, collective agency, self-expression. 

They are policy bundles, ethoses, and the disruption hits each differently.

This article is an attempt at mapping the terrain so that we can better route around the shifting conditions.

## Market signals & Prospective

A first thing to note is that AI job-market impact no longer seems hypothetical. It is difficult to disentangle from other pieces of context such as sustained high interest rates, post pandemic correction, or plain corporate optimization. What we see is a sharp decline in junior hiring in tech. Up to -73% YoY.[^1] New graduates now only represent 8% of hires.[^1] Seniors in contrast are still largely immune to the phenomenon.[^1] Code bases are increasingly AI-generated as opposed to manually written by diligent developers. As an example among others, Peter Steinberger single-handedly built OpenClaw, an unusually successful open-source project, by leveraging AI agents.[^2] In reaction to such developments, we are starting to see students changing course at a rate of 16%. 25% for technology students.[^3] They are understandably flocking towards AI/ML programs (+35% enrollment).[^4] It is important to note that these sectors are still growing, from an economic standpoints, despite reduced hiring suggesting some degree of de-correlation between growth and human labor.

![](/blog/assets/ethos-framework-ai-era/StockHiringRateDecorrelation.png)

I should also note that the technology is still developing and I expect further advancement in the coming years. In fact, DeepSeek published new research introducing **Engram**: a _conditional memory_ module that separates static knowledge lookup from dynamic reasoning.[^5] The core insight: current models do unnecessary work. Every time you ask a question, the model re-derives facts it already "knows" — burning expensive compute to reach the same answers it reached last time. Engram fixes this by giving the model a fast lookup table for stored facts, bypassing the expensive reasoning process entirely.⁵ Stop thinking about things you already know, just look them up. Models get faster, cheaper, better on long-context tasks. This is in line with a trend of frontier model costs falling roughly an order of magnitude per year, with no obvious efficiency ceiling in sight.[^6] Already, even with imperfect LLMs, brute-forcing problems with AI agents (throwing compute until tests pass) is a valid solution, and it should only become ever more so as prices keep falling.

Furthermore, Anthropic's own Claude Mythos Preview autonomously finds and exploits zero-day vulnerabilities across every major OS and browser, no human in the loop.[^7] 10 000 + found in testing. Rather than release it, Anthropic launched **Project Glasswing**: a restricted release to 50+ critical infrastructure organizations using it defensively to patch before comparable capabilities spread.[^7] A model that delivers working exploits overnight further compresses the role of developers whose output can be specified as a test suite.

## Alienation problem

These changes are certainly not small in magnitude. The effects on the psychology of developers has been notable. Where we might expect LLMs to reduce the workload and to overall be a quality-of-life upgrade, we see instead an erosion of the intrinsic motivation they used to experience in their job and increased rates of burnout. Where a developer could once focus on a single problem for a day, they now are moved through faster, and additional problems hit their plate immediately. Coupled with heightened productivity expectations, **cognitive load increases instead of diminishing**. Developers also experience a loss of attachment to the work. The reduction in local friction incidentally dilutes the highs. What gets done does not feel earned: **achievement without pride**. Loss of meaning.

This is not fundamentally a new problem. What gets expressed is the tension between the **Craft Ethos** and the **Industrial ethos**. The first is about judgement & excellence, mastery, long apprenticeship arcs, identity & skill pride, tacit knowledge and particularity. The second concerns itself with specs, throughput, repeatable processes, reliable execution, replaceability. Artisans from the industrialization era went through this exact transition.

The observed reaction pattern was defensiveness and resistance at first, then constraint-driven adaptation, with identity and pride detaching from the product. They relocated to attributes like reliability, speed, stamina instead. We also saw an eventual participation in unions and collective bargaining structures as a means of restoring agency: the awakening of a **Collective Ethos** at scale. The more complex the craft, the latter the conversion. Watchmaking wasn't industrialized until 1900+ but eventually it went through the transition too.[^8]

The creative industries, by necessity, have also been under pressure to balance these two aspects for the longest time. Audiences react to craft as the main value proposition, but doing so at scale requires an industrial ethos too. The creative industries were never able to reach the quality levels expected by audiences without skilled crafts people in the loop. Meanwhile, the technology sector always preached an industrial ethos but could never achieve it without the craft. We are now facing the risk of a full transition to a complete AI-enabled industrial ethos transition for both domains.

## Demand side/Reception

### AI technically equal or superior

Is AI output substitutable for human output ? We observe that general audiences cannot reliably distinguish AI versus human art in blind tests. They routinely misidentify provenance.[^9] AI generated code is found to be more readable than human written code more often than not.[^10] Visual art's provenance is only correctly identified at a rate of 50 to 60%, which is barely above chance.[^11] AI Music similarly seems to fool listeners 97% of the time.[^12] These statistics strongly suggest AI is at least on par with human actors on a technical level.

I will go further and say, AI should over time mechanistically out-perform artists and musicians from a technical standpoint at scale. The reason is that it is being trained directly on successful data and direct human feedback, a proxy for collective taste. An human expert's taste does not necessarily align with that of the general audience, in fact it often does not. Jazz Harmony or contrapuntal density are highly valued by high level musicians yet underappreciated by the general population, who prefers simpler Pop music. Similarly, audiences might admire surface level traits such as patient rendering and detailing in observation drawings, and under-appreciate structural skills that are harder to develop but less obvious for non practitioners, such as multi-points perspective or solid forms from imagination. This gap suggests to me that taste alignment should come a lot easier for Ais than it does for individuals.

### Anti-AI bias, pressure for innovation, human moat

However there is a moat. When provenance is labeled, preferences shifts strongly in favor of human made art and music. AI generations do not carry the same "celebration of human potential" innate story. We value the effort itself, which is a large part of why stop motion movies like Kubo and the Two Strings elicit such a strong response despite being an inefficient path to the result by modern standards. The meaning behind the work being tied to the creators creates a strong anti-AI bias. **Substitution works technically but collapses culturally** the moment the source is disclosed. That bias is why Cara exists and gained popularity: an Artstation/Instagram hybrid social media feed for artists with an explicit no-AI rule in portfolios.[^13] That's also why Chess players are still largely appreciated despite having been out-competed by Chess engines for a while already now. That's also why AI users fail to obtain similar respect even when putting out similar outputs. Below is the work of Juliette Brocal & co: 100% hand-made.

![](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EH56ljIb3eE)

I must note this same anti-AI bias and attribution problem is probably part of the reason why we see junior developers struggling to get in. **One would expect AI tools to shrink the gap between a junior and a senior**, making juniors a better value proposition overall than they were before when compared against more expensive senior devs. But **instead we see a collapse in junior positions** while seniors are still able to switch jobs and get hired with relative ease. The reason is possibly that because it's understood that seniors got where they are through craft and hand-written code, respect is more easily given. Juniors on the other hand might be capable of producing similar outputs when using AI tools, but because of the anti-AI bias, the value of the work is deemed lower when judged on Craft ethos. Pre AI-era and hand-written work is thus disproportionately valuable when it comes to signaling, even though increasingly the on-the-job expectation is to work as an LLM operator under Industrial ethos, rather than as a Craft person.

My interest was also piqued when I saw the band Angine de Poitrine blew up online. It is a guitar & bass + looper and drums duet with the particularity of using 24 EDO instruments. That is to say, the guitar and bass have 24 frets per octave instead of the usual 12, giving the band access to quarter tones, a smaller unit interval than is typically used. The music is weird, full of what our ears with all their priors would read as dissonant. I wouldn't have bet that general audiences would take a concerted interest in microtonal music, and yet here we are. I interpret it as an immune reaction to AI art. Microtonal music is almost entirely out of the training datasets. This is not music that can be AI generated today. It is unmistakably human made. I foresee this pattern occuring more and more. I expect faster habituation and an increased need for structural novelty (as opposed to surface novelty) from audiences as AI floods markets with infinite variations of the same thing. Here, AI acts as a forcing function towards more innovation and creative R&D on the Craft side in the same way that the invention of the photograph pushed painter towards inventing new visual languages such as Impressionism, Post Impressionism, Expressionism or Cubism. Hopefully this means other tuning systems such as 31 EDO should see their time come sooner rather than later (see Mike Battaglia's performance). I should also note that tools that unlock entirely new creative areas to be explored seem like they might become particularly valuable for that purpose (in this example, 24 or 31 EDO instruments).

## Economic limits

The caveat however: **appreciation is not necessarily the same as economic viability**. Professional chess players remain a tiny crowd. Similarly professional seats for sports people are an incredibly tiny fraction of the total number of people who partake in sports. Artists in general have a long history already of being both celebrated and economically punished in their journeys even before AI. It is possible to have both a market for human art that receives a premium valuation while also have that same market shrink in size, where only the few top artists get paid. Audience attention doesn't look like it can grow as fast or faster than the supply of art given AI generations flood. This commodification is at the heart of the economic problem.

A parallel I keep thinking of, is that of reading and writing. Those were once commercial skills under the Scribe descriptor. Since then, the Scribe profession went extinct but reading and writing didn't lose any of their intrinsic value. In fact we collectively valued these skills so much that we made sure every one could do them which is how the skills got commodified. Today, reading and writing are embedded in most human activities. However, people who get paid mainly for their writing are rare. They fragmented into fiction or didactic authors, copywriters, technical writers, journalists and others. The value is embedded in what one is writing about rather than the simple fact of being able to write. Originality, analysis, persuasion or judgment are the real differentiators. Text has become the medium, rather than an end in itself. Similarly AI tools might expand coding, or creative baseline capability, which mean these job might once again fragment into new presentations where creativity and judgement will matter more than raw execution. This also reinforces the idea that roles might increasingly move towards R&D efforts as opposed to linear execution.

It is worth flagging that anything R&D is a high variance endeavor. Most research is commercially fruitless. In medicinal science, 90% of medicine candidates fail to reach commercial release.[^14] This is similar to the odds for entrepreneurship wherein 90% of startups eventually fail.[^15] The successful bets make up for the failed ones. Although I do expect the R&D role to become more prevalent as AI become increasingly capable of executing known methods and scaling findings, I also reckon we might face a bit of a problem if we expect individuals to absorb the variance. What were the odds of Angine de Poitrine's proposition being successful, as an example, when judged before the fact rather than in retrospect ? Is it reasonable to anchor one's financial trajectory on such a bet ? Probably not, and that's also true for scientific research. Yet do we think it's worth having people conducting such research ? Yes, which is why we institutionalized the effort and why scientists get public backing. Similar public funding mechanisms exist in the creative fields, but I suspect the subject might eventually become more widespread than just science and art. I expect there is going to be a lot of work worth doing in a fully realized AI-era future as the frontier will keep expending and we keep wanting to know what's beyond it, but a lot of it will look like low probability of success bets. The funding of such bets is poorly incentivized at local scale but is invaluable at global scale. This problem will need an answer.

## The Scientific Ethos & Expressive Ethos

So how should we adapt to this new board ? How should we use, or not use AI ?

On the ethics of using AI, there are obviously large elephants in the room. Modern generative AI systems were built on the internet which is a proxy for the sum of accumulated human knowledge and creative expression. The original authors of the material used for training have not been compensated. AI companies are planning to capture value that is the fruit of a multi-generational collective effort. Credit where credit is due, they are technical enablers, which is not a trivial achievement. But the core value proposition, the intelligence of these models, should, in my opinion, be considered a commons and be governed as a public infrastructure. In practice this would mean capped profits for these companies after which the surplus goes to the public. It is culture in compressed form: our collective inheritance. In that sense, using gen AI for one's own work is ethical in the same way that scientific research is expected to build on previous work or open-source software builds on itself. That's the third ethos that I want to invoke in this article: the **Scientific Ethos**. Arts themselves under a certain lens are actually a multi generational research project subject to the Scientific ethos. A figure like Bach wouldn't have wanted his composition frameworks to die with him. He would have wanted for them to be used and for further generations to move beyond them. Similarly a Chick Corea's body of work is an extension to a shared formal language in jazz music, a generative system.

The difficulty lies in the fact that in the same basket you also inadvertently cannibalize work from authors that would have fallen strictly under an **Expressive Ethos**. Which is about subjectivity, sincerity of vision, inner necessity, and self-expression as primary drivers. The celebration of unicity and unfungibility. Think Vincent Van Gogh, Munch, Bacon. Or Kurt Cobain, Jimi Hendrix. The value of these artists' art however, is not fully in the dataset. Remove the authors and the bits left by themselves do very little. Imitation when judged against an Expressive ethos, is completely devoid of value. The risk for such artists is not devaluation by imitation but overcrowding leading to an erosion of discoverability. Established Expressive types are unlikely to lose their audiences to the fact AI can imitate their surface output. Their value is not in the artifact but in the link it provides to the person behind it. Remove it and the proposition falls apart. 
## Legitimacy & Trust

If we were to conclude that AI generation is a potentially potent tool that could complement our workflows, or that the ambient productivity pressures justify using it in commercial contexts, then we have to answer the question of how to supply legitimacy and trust when authorship gets diluted and when the artist's hand gets buried. Fortunately that question has been largely answered by pop stars and the producer-musician archetype. It is typical for such auteurs to release highly produced tracks with many names to their credits: distributing the load across many individuals. How then can an audience remain confident that the top-liner can stand on their own and is deserving of their trust ? The solution seem to be to periodically release deliberately stripped down, minimal, often acoustic, songs and performances. Below is a Lady Gaga song, in its fully produced version first, and then one of several intimate minimal renditions of that same track that she released.

![Lady Gaga - Disease (Official Music Video)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fmC6b6_ovZY) ![Lady Gaga - Disease (The Poison Live)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Et31X1n5kR8)

An other pattern that's commonly seen, is to use AI heavily for what it's good at – for example search and drafting at scale – but to filter it through a human layer before presenting its output. Youtubers in the AI space, such as David Shapiro, Nate B Jones or are early adopters and rely heavily on AI at pre-production stage. However, they have not attempted to automate the delivery yet. They are still recording themselves and talking to the camera, old-school style; not generating a video avatar or an artificial voice. This is not for lack of know-how. They’ve empirically discovered that the human interface layer is genuinely valuable. Filtering, interjecting their own thoughts and reflections, establishing positioning and skin-in-the-game, providing temporal coherence and accumulated narrative, displaying tone adjustments and micro expressions as authenticators… The presenter, by showing up models that the information presented matters, without which an audience is unable to care.

These observations fit into **Costly Signaling Theory**, a model developed by economist Michael Spence in 1973. **When a primary output becomes easy to produce**, such as produced music or AI-drafted text, **legitimacy migrates away from it**, towards artifacts that remain asymmetrically expensive to counterfeit. The stripped acoustic performance is costly for those dependent on production. Trained musicians can produce it comparatively cheaply. The human presentation layer in Youtube videos functions similarly: the presenter stakes his accumulated reputation, an asymmetric cost that an automated AI pipeline can’t bear. A signal with a liability attached to it registers differently as one without. 

## Recommendations

In light of these reflections, it seems to me that the answer to AI disruption is not black & white. A body should not be stuck in either a sympathetic-dominant (high alertness) mode nor a parasympathethc (hypoarousal) mode. The body should be adaptive and able to switch from one state to the other. You want high alertness during a productive day, and hypoarousal/downregulation in the evening as you get ready for bed. Failure comes not from any of those state in isolation, but rather from an inability to switch and match the appropriate state to its corresponding context. 
In this article I laid a bit of a framework around the idea of ethoses. They can be though of as rough policy bundles. 

AI tools feed directly into the Industrial Ethos. They offer scalability and velocity and are becoming increasingly common as productivity enhancers in the workplace. Eventually, not being able to use these tools might read as a liability when the productivity baseline expectation shift up. It is psychologically important to separate Industrial logic from Craft logic and to not get them mixed up as it can be cause for burnout. The Industrial Ethos is productive and powerful but does not protect by itself against fungibility. **Necessary, but insufficient.** 

The Craft Ethos is the one most directly under threat as AI generators are increasingly able to imitate its output. Craft lacks the scale and speed that the Industrial Ethos can afford and risks getting out-competed, practically. That being said, Craft still has disproportionate signaling power. Trust is extended more readily to those who can hand-build, even though the on-the-job day-to-day operation might increasingly rely on AI tools. Costly Signaling Theory suggests that **legitimacy might move out of the primary output itself (now increasingly AI augmented) towards the hand-crafted version of it.** 

The Scientific Ethos is becoming increasingly relevant. As execution bottleneck are removed and less and less people are needed to carry the executive burden, what remains is frontier work. There is no shortage of frontier work to be made as the frontier keeps moving and what’s beyond is infinite. **Demonstrated R&D capability and initiative might become a growing asset**. Research-type work, at this point in time though, suffer from a funding problem given widespread and justified variance aversion.

This leads us to the Collective Ethos. When and if the time comes, **the correct move might eventually become to partake in collective movements and advocate in favor of treating AI models as commons**, and in favor of redistribution solutions to protect the individuals against the high variance that could become common ground in a future where routine work is commoditized. In a way not dissimilar to previous waves or industrialization. These movements in the past were responsible for some of the greatest social advances we made, and such an opportunity might present itself again. 

Then the Expressive Ethos, I think, doesn’t really go out of fashion. Arts, or even online media dynamics, teach us that **the value is in fact largely in the person, rather than the proof-of-work** only. In fact the artifact is evidence of the existence of a subject more so than anything else. **Non-fungibility is the point. And its generator is authenticity.** It suffers from a discoverability problem, but can’t be ignored when seen. Once a matching tribe is found, the authentic one is not substitutable.

But the last one standing, is probably going to be the **Contemplative Ethos**. Stephen Hawking, once answered, as he was nearing the end of his life, paralyzed and only able to communicate by twitching a few muscles, when asked for a word of wisdom: “wow”. **Embracing the magnificence of experience itself, indifferent of outcomes, remains and will always remain a very worthwhile posture**. 

**Being able to route around these policies responsively, with full awareness of the terrain, might be the only workable response** to the shifting board.
